# Hedge-Fund-Style Stock Analysis Guide

This guide is educational. It is not personal financial advice, a buy/sell signal, or a substitute for a licensed advisor. A strong investor process is about disciplined evidence, risk control, and repeatable decisions.

## Core Mindset

Think like a portfolio manager, not a stock fan.

Every stock should answer four questions:

1. What is the business worth?
2. What does the market currently believe?
3. What could make the market change its mind?
4. What could make me wrong, and how bad could the loss be?

Your goal is not to find exciting companies. Your goal is to find situations where the expected return justifies the risk, the evidence is strong enough, and the position fits the portfolio.

## The 10-Step Research Workflow

### 1. Define the Mandate

Before analyzing the company, define the job of the stock.

- Long-term compounder
- Turnaround
- Cyclical recovery
- Special situation
- Deep value
- Growth at reasonable price
- Speculative catalyst
- Pair trade or peer-relative idea

Also define the time horizon:

- 3 months: catalyst or market behavior matters more.
- 1-2 years: earnings revisions, valuation, and execution matter most.
- 3-5 years: business quality, reinvestment runway, and capital allocation dominate.

### 2. First-Pass Screen

Use your app to quickly sort names.

Start with:

- Readiness
- Data Confidence Flag
- Score
- Overall Flag
- Peer Group
- Market Cap
- Revenue
- FCF
- Cash
- Debt
- Current Ratio
- Dilution 1Y / 3Y
- Momentum Flag
- Drawdown Flag
- Volatility Flag

Decision:

- Green: worth deeper work.
- Yellow: watch, needs context.
- Purple: speculative or catalyst-only.
- Gray: fix data before trusting the output.
- Red: skip unless there is an exceptional reason.

### 3. Understand the Business

Read the business section of the latest 10-K or annual report.

Answer:

- What does the company sell?
- Who are the customers?
- Why do customers buy it?
- Is demand recurring, cyclical, regulated, or one-time?
- What are the key cost drivers?
- What are the main competitors?
- What could make the product obsolete?
- Is the company a price maker or price taker?

Hedge-fund-style test:

If you cannot explain how the company makes money in five sentences, do not model it yet.

### 4. Read the Filing Like an Analyst

Use SEC filings as the source of truth. For U.S. companies, start with:

- Latest 10-K
- Latest 10-Q
- Recent 8-Ks
- Proxy statement for executive compensation and share issuance
- Earnings call transcript
- Investor presentation, but treat it as management marketing

Focus on:

- Business
- Risk Factors
- MD&A
- Financial Statements
- Notes to the financial statements
- Liquidity and capital resources
- Debt maturities
- Segment reporting
- Customer concentration
- Legal proceedings

Do not only read the numbers. Read what changed.

### 5. Build the Historical Picture

Use 3-5 years of history.

Track:

- Revenue growth
- Gross margin trend
- Operating margin trend
- Net income trend
- Operating cash flow
- Capex
- Free cash flow
- Cash
- Debt
- Current ratio
- Share count
- Dilution
- SBC as percentage of revenue

Interpretation:

- Revenue growing while margins expand: strong.
- Revenue growing but FCF worsening: investigate quality of growth.
- Margin compression: check pricing pressure, input costs, competition, or mix shift.
- Cash falling with negative FCF: runway risk.
- Debt rising faster than cash flow: balance sheet risk.
- Share count rising: dilution risk.

### 6. Judge Business Quality

High-quality companies usually show some mix of:

- Durable revenue growth
- High or improving gross margins
- Positive operating leverage
- Positive FCF
- Strong balance sheet
- Low dilution
- Pricing power
- Recurring revenue or repeat demand
- High returns on capital
- Management that allocates capital well

Weak companies often show:

- Revenue growth dependent on acquisitions
- Constant restructuring charges
- Negative FCF with no clear path to profitability
- Heavy dilution
- Rising debt
- Customer concentration
- Margin deterioration
- Management changing metrics often
- Big gap between adjusted earnings and GAAP earnings

### 7. Compare Against Peers

A stock is rarely cheap or expensive in isolation.

Compare:

- EV/Revenue
- EV/FCF
- P/S
- P/E, if earnings are meaningful
- Gross margin
- Operating margin
- FCF margin
- Current ratio
- Net cash or net debt
- Growth rate
- Dilution
- Momentum and drawdown

Ask:

- Is it cheaper because it is lower quality?
- Is it expensive because it deserves a premium?
- Is the market ignoring an improving trend?
- Is one peer distorting the median?
- Are peers actually comparable?

### 8. Analyze Market Behavior

Momentum, drawdown, volatility, and returns are useful because they show how the market is treating the stock.

Use them this way:

- Momentum: confirms whether investors are already rewarding the story.
- Drawdown: shows how much pain is already priced in.
- Volatility: helps judge position size and risk.
- 1M / 3M / 6M / 1Y returns: shows whether performance is accelerating or fading.

Examples:

- Great fundamentals + positive momentum: potential compounder or crowded winner.
- Great fundamentals + deep drawdown: possible opportunity, but check what broke.
- Weak fundamentals + positive momentum: possible hype or catalyst trade.
- Cheap valuation + negative momentum: possible value trap.
- High volatility + weak balance sheet: position sizing must be conservative.

### 9. Identify the Variant View

This is where hedge-fund thinking really starts.

A good thesis needs a reason the market may be wrong.

Write:

- Consensus view: what the market seems to believe.
- My variant view: what I believe differently.
- Evidence: what supports my view.
- Catalyst: what could force the market to reprice it.
- Timeline: when the thesis should start working.
- Kill criteria: what proves I am wrong.

Examples of variant views:

- Margins will recover faster than expected.
- A new product cycle is underappreciated.
- Cash burn is less dangerous than the market thinks.
- A business segment is worth more than the current market cap implies.
- Peer valuation gap is too wide.
- The company is structurally worse than bulls believe.

### 10. Make the Decision

Use four buckets:

- Pass: not enough upside, too much uncertainty, or bad data.
- Watchlist: interesting but needs a better price, catalyst, or cleaner data.
- Deep Dive: strong enough for a full memo and valuation work.
- Candidate Position: thesis, risk/reward, catalyst, and sizing are clear.

Never buy only because a score is high. The score tells you where to look. The memo decides what to do.

## Scorecard

Use a 1-5 score for each category.

| Category | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Business Quality | Declining or fragile | Stable but average | Durable, high-quality, improving |
| Growth | Shrinking | Modest or uneven | Strong and durable |
| Margins | Weak or worsening | Average | High or expanding |
| FCF | Negative, unclear path | Mixed | Strong and recurring |
| Balance Sheet | Liquidity risk | Manageable | Net cash or low leverage |
| Dilution | Heavy | Moderate | Low or buybacks |
| Valuation | Expensive vs quality | Fair | Cheap vs quality and peers |
| Momentum | Breaking down | Mixed | Positive confirmation |
| Drawdown | Broken trend | Normal pullback | Opportunity with intact thesis |
| Catalyst | None | Possible | Clear and time-bound |
| Data Quality | Missing/stale | Usable | Complete and fresh |
| Risk/Reward | Poor | Acceptable | Asymmetric |

Suggested interpretation:

- 50-60: high-quality candidate.
- 40-49: deep dive or watchlist.
- 30-39: needs a very specific catalyst.
- Below 30: usually pass.

## Red Flags

Be careful when you see:

- Revenue up but cash flow down for multiple years.
- Adjusted earnings positive while GAAP losses continue.
- Rising share count with no clear value creation.
- Debt rising while margins fall.
- Customer concentration without pricing power.
- Frequent acquisitions masking organic weakness.
- Management changing KPIs when old KPIs weaken.
- Large related-party transactions.
- Weak liquidity with high volatility.
- Stock down heavily while fundamentals are also worsening.
- Data gaps in core fields like revenue, cash, debt, share count, and FCF.

## Weekly Routine

1. Refresh data for watchlist names.
2. Sort by Score and Overall Flag.
3. Check names with improving readiness or new green/yellow flags.
4. Review price trend changes: momentum, drawdown, volatility.
5. Pick 1-3 names for deeper filing review.
6. Update memos only when something material changes.
7. Re-rank the watchlist.
8. Remove names where the thesis is dead or data is too weak.

## How To Use This App

Use the app as your first-pass analyst.

Start here:

- Overview: find the best candidates and warning names.
- Master Watchlist: compare color-coded score, flags, market behavior, valuation, and readiness.
- Score Details: audit why each component score was assigned and how much it contributes to the overall score.
- Data Quality: check whether important fields are complete, partial, stale, or missing before trusting a screen.
- Peer Comparison: decide whether valuation and quality are attractive relative to similar companies.
- Historical Fundamentals: inspect multi-year operating trends.
- Price Trends: inspect market behavior and risk.
- Price History: audit raw candle data.
- API Cache: debug raw SEC/Finnhub fields.
- Model Readiness: check missing fields before trusting conclusions.

The app should tell you what deserves attention. Your filing review and investment memo should decide whether the idea is real.
